# Adi Parva 03 — Paushya Parva

Source: Mahabharata Volume 1.pdf. Verse numbers are this book's. Read from OCR, with quoted verses checked against the page images.

## Chapter 3 — Sarama's curse on Janamejaya, Somashrava made priest, the guru-devotion of Aruni, Upamanyu, Veda and Uttanka, and Uttanka urging Janamejaya to the snake sacrifice

PDF pp. 135-164. 188 numbered units, much of it Sanskrit prose.

### Shravana

**Sarama's curse (vv. 1-11).** Janamejaya, son of Parikshit, is holding a long sacrifice at Kurukshetra with his brothers Shrutasena, Ugrasena and Bhimasena. Sarameya, son of the gods' bitch Sarama, comes there, and the brothers beat him. He runs crying to his mother. She first assumes he must have done wrong. He says he neither looked at nor licked the offerings. Sarama goes to the sacrifice and asks why her innocent son was struck. They give no answer. She says: because he was beaten without offence, an unforeseen fear will come on you. Janamejaya is shaken, and after the sacrifice he looks hard for a priest who can quiet this curse.

**Somashrava (vv. 12-20).** Hunting in his own land, Janamejaya finds the ashram of the sage Shrutashrava, whose son Somashrava is devoted to tapas (austerity). Shrutashrava says this son was born of a she-snake who drank his semen, and raised by his tapas. He can quell every curse-force except one sent by Mahadeva. He has one private rule: he gives any Brahmin whatever is asked. Janamejaya accepts, tells his brothers to obey the priest without question, and goes off to conquer Takshashila.

**Aruni (vv. 21-33).** Now the story turns to the sage Ayoda Dhaumya and his three students. He sends Aruni of Panchala to repair a broken field-bank. Aruni can't stop the water, thinks of a way, and lies down in the gap himself. Later Dhaumya asks where he is, goes there with the others and calls. Aruni gets up at once, breaking the bank, and asks what to do next. Dhaumya names him Uddalaka, from breaking through (uddalana), and says that since he obeyed, all the Vedas and dharma-shastras will shine in his mind.

**Upamanyu (vv. 33-77).** Told to guard the cows, he returns each evening and bows. Dhaumya sees he is plump and asks how he lives. Each answer is closed off in turn:
1. He lives on alms: he must hand them over.
2. He begs a second round: that is greed and takes from other beggars.
3. He drinks the cows' milk: not permitted.
4. He drinks the foam calves spit out: that harms the calves, who spit more out of pity for him.

Starving in the forest, he eats arka leaves, goes blind, wanders and falls into a well. At sunset Dhaumya says, "I've closed off all his food, so he must be angry," and goes to search, calling him. Told what happened, Dhaumya tells him to praise the Ashvins, physicians of the gods. Upamanyu's hymn (vv. 57-68) calls them first-born, and speaks of 360 cows and one calf, a wheel of 720 spokes, a wheel of twelve spokes, and asks for release. They offer him a cake. He won't eat without first offering it to his teacher. They say his teacher ate theirs without offering it to his own guru, so do likewise. He refuses again. Pleased, they say: your teacher's teeth are black iron, yours will be gold, your sight will return. He goes back and tells all. Dhaumya is pleased and says the Vedas and dharma-shastras will shine in him.

**Veda (vv. 78-81).** Told to stay and serve, he lives long in the guru's house, yoked like an ox to heavy loads, bearing cold, heat, hunger and thirst, and never objecting. In time the guru is satisfied, and he gains welfare and all-knowledge. When Veda later has his own three students, he never orders them to work or serve, because he knows the misery of life in a guru's house.

**Uttanka and the guru's wife (vv. 82-92).** Janamejaya and Paushya, both Kshatriyas (warrior class), choose Veda as their teacher. Going away, Veda tells his student Uttanka to make good anything lacking at home. The women of the house say the teacher's wife is in her fertile season and is grieving, so he should see that it doesn't go to waste. Uttanka refuses: he won't do a wrong thing on women's word, and the teacher never told him to do wrong. Veda returns, is pleased, and gives him leave. Uttanka insists on a guru's fee, quoting that when teaching or asking is done without dharma, one of the two dies or comes to hate. Veda tells him to ask his wife.

**The earrings (vv. 96-125).** She asks for the earrings worn by King Paushya's queen, in four days, for a rite at which she will serve Brahmins. On the road a huge man on a huge bull tells him to eat the bull's dung, saying his teacher ate it too. He does, also the urine, and rinses his mouth standing up in his hurry. Paushya sends him into the inner rooms but he can't see the queen. Paushya says he must be impure, because she is too chaste to be seen by the impure. Uttanka recalls rinsing standing and does it properly, seated and facing east. The queen then appears, gives the earrings gladly, and warns that Takshaka wants them. Paushya asks him to stay for a shraddha (rite for the dead). The food is cold with a hair in it. Uttanka curses him to go blind. Paushya curses him to be childless for fouling good food. On checking, the food really was made by a woman with loose hair. Paushya apologises. Uttanka says he'll be blind, then soon cured, and asks Paushya to lift his curse too. Paushya says he can't, as his anger won't settle. Uttanka replies that the curse was conditional on the food being clean, so it doesn't hold.

**Takshaka and the Naga world (vv. 126-159).** A naked mendicant keeps appearing and vanishing. When Uttanka sets the earrings down to do his water rites, the mendicant takes them and runs. Uttanka catches him, and he becomes Takshaka and enters a hole. Uttanka digs with his staff. Indra sends the vajra into the staff, and it opens the way. Uttanka praises the Nagas in verses and gets nothing. He sees two women weaving with black and white threads, a twelve-spoked wheel turned by six boys, a man and a splendid horse. He praises them. The man offers a boon. Uttanka asks that the Nagas come under his control. He is told to blow into the horse's anus. Fire and smoke pour from it, the Naga world fills, and Takshaka brings out the earrings. It is the fourth day and he's far away, so the man has him ride the horse. The teacher's wife is about to curse him when he arrives, and she welcomes him instead.

**What it all meant (vv. 160-170).** Veda explains: the women are Dhata and Vidhata, the threads night and day, the boys the six seasons, the wheel the year. The man is Parjanya, the horse Agni, the bull the Naga king Airavata, its rider Indra. The dung was amrita (nectar), which is why Uttanka didn't die among the Nagas. Indra is Veda's friend and helped out of compassion. Veda gives him leave.

**Uttanka and Janamejaya (vv. 170-188).** Uttanka, angry and wanting to repay Takshaka, goes to Hastinapura. He tells the victorious king he is doing the wrong task, childishly. Janamejaya asks what is needed. Uttanka: the task is yours. Takshaka killed your innocent father, turned Kashyapa back, and is proud with strength. Burn him in a snake sacrifice, quickly. That will repay your father and greatly please me, since he obstructed my guru's errand. The king blazes up like fire fed with ghee, and asks his ministers about his father's going to heaven, in Uttanka's presence, sunk in grief.

**Differs from the popular version:**
- People commonly take the snake sacrifice as Janamejaya's own idea. Here Uttanka proposes it, and he says he has a grudge of his own.
- Aruni is told only to repair the bank. Lying in the gap is his own idea.
- Dhaumya is often assumed to be just a harsh test-giver. Here he goes searching and calls, and the Ashvins say he himself failed the test Upamanyu passes.
- The disgusting food on the road turns out to be amrita.

**Unclear, flagged:**
- Sarama's curse is left unresolved in this chapter, and Somashrava's rule about Brahmins is set up and then not used.
- v. 82: Janamejaya chooses Veda as teacher, after v. 14 has him choose Somashrava as priest. The text doesn't say whether this is the same Janamejaya.
- vv. 57-68: the hymn is obscure. The Hindi gloss gives a heavy spiritual reading (for example v. 59, the swallowed quail read as the soul and time), and I can't tell how much is in the Sanskrit. I summarised rather than retold it.
- vv. 144, 146, 61 and 163: spoke counts differ, twelve, 360 and 720, depending on what is being counted.
- v. 167: the Sanskrit says Parjanya; the Hindi adds "(Indra)". v. 168 also makes the bull's rider Indra. So Indra may be both figures, but the text doesn't say so directly.
- vv. 187-188: Janamejaya reacts as though hearing of his father's death for the first time, though he has been king for some time.
- v. 97: "punyaka" is treated as a rite by the Hindi. No details are given.

### Manana

#### Episode: Sarama and the silent brothers

- **L3 Psychological:** Sarama's first move is fair: she assumes her son did something. Only after asking does she come. The brothers' silence is what provokes the curse, more than the blow.
- **L4 Ethical:** Dharma-sukshma: a sacred rite does not excuse cruelty done around it. The dog never touched the offering, so the brothers' purity is protected from nothing. Having no answer when asked why is itself the failure.
- **L5 Literary:** A small domestic scene, a pup crying to its mother, opens the chapter that ends in a call to burn all snakes. Unprovoked harm comes first; the rest is a chain.

#### Episode: Aruni, Upamanyu and Veda

- **L2 Adhyatmika:** The three show different kinds of giving up. Aruni gives his body to the task. Upamanyu gives up every source of comfort. Veda bears the load for years. In each, what is sought seems to arrive without being studied: "the Vedas will shine in you."
- **L3 Psychological:** Upamanyu never argues. He says "tatha" (so be it) every time and goes on guarding. When Dhaumya says he must be angry, he has misjudged him. The student's inner state is better than his teacher's guess.
- **L4 Ethical:** The hard part is that the text praises obedience, then shows its cost: a student blind in a well. Veda draws the conclusion himself, refusing to put his own students through it (v. 81). The chapter does not condemn Dhaumya, but it does let Veda's choice stand beside him.
- **L5 Literary:** The Upamanyu section works by patient repetition. The same question, the same "you are still fat", the same bow. Each closed door is funnier and more alarming than the last, until the well.
- **L6 Historical & textual:** The Hindi bracket at v. 21 presents these as lessons on obeying a guru. In the Sanskrit they just start, with no such framing. The line "this was his test" (vv. 77, 80) is in the Sanskrit.

#### Episode: Uttanka, the road and the kingdom of snakes

- **L1 Theological:** Divine help comes disguised and unannounced: as a stranger pressing dung on him, a vajra hidden in a staff, a horse. Uttanka learns who they were only afterwards. The hymn calls the man the lord of the three worlds, who separates truth from falsehood.
- **L2 Adhyatmika:** The thing I recoil from on the road may be what keeps me alive later. A small slip also matters. A rinse done standing up and in a hurry is enough to make the queen invisible, until he slows down and does it properly.
- **L3 Psychological:** Uttanka is quick in everything: quick to curse over a hair, quick to boast Takshaka won't dare, quick to set the earrings down. Paushya's anger he can't lift, and he says so plainly. Both are honest about their tempers.
- **L4 Ethical:** His refusal of the guru's wife is clear-eyed: an order to fill what's lacking doesn't cover a wrong act. Obedience is not unlimited here, and Veda is pleased by the refusal. That qualifies the earlier stories.
- **L5 Literary:** Adbhuta rasa (wonder) with some comedy, the hair in the food, the horse's rear. The weaving women with black and white threads are the chapter's still moment, and Veda's plain decoding of the images ends the strangeness.

Verse (v. 91, p. 149): *yaś cādharmeṇa vai brūyād yaś cādharmeṇa pṛcchati / tayor anyataraḥ praiti vidveṣaṃ cādhigacchati* — "Whoever speaks without dharma and whoever asks without dharma: one of the two departs and comes to hatred."

Verse (v. 123, p. 154): *navanītaṃ hṛdayaṃ brāhmaṇasya / vāci kṣuro nihitas tīkṣṇadhāraḥ / tad ubhayam etad viparītaṃ kṣatriyasya / vāṅ navanītaṃ hṛdayaṃ tīkṣṇadhāram* — "A Brahmin's heart is butter, and in his speech is set a sharp-edged razor. Both are reversed in a Kshatriya: speech is butter, the heart sharp-edged."

#### Episode: Uttanka lights the fire

- **L3 Psychological:** v. 170 gives the motive first: he is angry and wants to repay Takshaka. Only then does he come to the king and speak of the king's father. His own grievance appears near the end (vv. 184-185). The king's grief is real, and Uttanka uses it.
- **L4 Ethical:** He got the earrings back, and his errand succeeded. A whole race is to burn for it. A wise man's speech can be right in fact (Parikshit dead, Kashyapa turned back) and still be fuel. The text's own image is ghee on fire (v. 186).
- **L5 Literary:** Raudra rasa (fury) enters at the close. The student who was so humble and so careful in the first half ends as the one who incites. Nobody in the text points this out, which leaves it to the reader.

### Nididhyasana

Before acting today on someone's clearly justified anger, including my own, ask what grievance of the speaker's is riding on it.
