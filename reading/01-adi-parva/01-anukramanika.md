# Adi Parva 01 — Anukramanika Parva (the Table of Contents section)

Source: Mahabharata Volume 1.pdf. Verse numbers are this book's. Read from OCR, with quoted verses checked against the page images.

## Chapter 1 — Opening of the work, a brief list of most of its topics, and the glory of reading it

PDF pp. 27-55 and 64-83. Pp. 56-63 are colour plates inserted mid-chapter, not text. The closing note counts 282 verses, including 7 extra verses of the southern recension (dakshinatya, the South Indian manuscript line).

### Shravana

**Invocation (before v. 1).** Bow to Narayana, to Nara, best of men, to the goddess Sarasvati and to Vyasa, and only then recite the Jaya ("Victory", here a name for the Mahabharata). The translator's note reads Narayana as Krishna and Nara as Arjuna. Further salutations follow to Vasudeva, Brahma, the Prajapatis (progenitors), Krishna Dvaipayana (Vyasa) and the Vinayakas (removers of obstacles).

**The frame (vv. 1-21).** At Naimisharanya forest, Shaunaka is running a twelve-year sacrifice. Ugrashrava, son of Lomaharshana, a Suta (bard) and Purana expert, comes to the sages at their leisure, out of curiosity and in humility. They surround him for stories. Asked where he has been, he says he heard the Mahabharata recited by Vaishampayana at Janamejaya's snake sacrifice. He then toured tirthas (sacred places) and visited Samantapanchaka, where the Kuru-Pandava war was fought. He offers Puranas or histories, and the sages ask for Vyasa's Bharata as Vaishampayana told it.

**Salutation and creation (vv. 22-47).** Ugrashrava bows to Vishnu as Hari and Hrishikesha, identified with Brahman (the Absolute), beyond existent and non-existent. Poets have told this history, are telling it and will tell it again. In the darkness at the start, one huge egg appeared, the seed of all beings, with Brahman inside it. From it came Brahma, Rudra, Manu, Daksha and his seven sons, twenty-one Prajapatis, gods, spirits, sages, kings, the elements and time. Everything dissolves at the age's end and returns, like a season's signs, in a wheel with no start or end. The gods number 33,333 in brief. A solar line runs through Vivasvan, Devabhrat and Subhrat to three sons with 10,000, 100,000 and 1,000,000 sons of their own, and from them the Kuru, Yadu, Bharata, Yayati and Ikshvaku lines.

**What Vyasa saw and how the text is opened (vv. 48-54).** Vyasa perceived all this and the Vedas, yoga, dharma, artha (wealth and purpose) and kama (desire). He told it both in brief and at length, because the learned like both. Readers begin the text at three different points: "Manu" (the Hindi says "Narayanam namaskritya"), Astika, or Uparichara. A parenthetical southern passage shows Vyasa in a Himalayan cave, purified and seated on kusha grass, seeing the whole work through yoga.

**Brahma and Ganesha (vv. 55-83).** Vyasa finishes the work in his mind and wonders how to teach it. Brahma comes in person. Vyasa lists the contents: the Vedas' secret, Vedangas and Upanishads, past, present and future, old age and death, dharma and the ashramas (life stages), the four varnas (social classes), astronomy, logic, medicine, tirthas, warfare and city-building. His worry is that no one on earth can write it down. Brahma calls him the foremost sage, says his word has always been true, so "kavya" (poem) it shall be, and tells him to call on Ganesha. Ganesha agrees if his pen never stops for a moment. Vyasa counters that he must write nothing without understanding it. Ganesha says "Om". Vyasa then knots the text with hard verses, and while the all-knowing Ganesha pauses over one, Vyasa composes many more.

**The images (vv. 84-93).** The Bharata is an eye-salve stick, a sun, a full moon and a lamp. It is also a tree: the summary chapter is the seed, Pauloma and Astika the root, Sambhava the trunk, Sabha and Aranya the nest-hollows, Arani the knot, Virata and Udyoga the heartwood, Bhishma the great branch, Drona the leaves, Karna the white flowers, Shalya the scent, Stri and Aishika the shade, Shanti the great fruit, Ashvamedha the nectar, Ashramavasika the resting place, and Mausala the crown. All poets will live off it as beings live off rain.

**Editions of the text (vv. 94-109).** At his mother's and Bhishma's command, Vyasa fathers Dhritarashtra, Pandu and Vidura in Vichitravirya's "field" (wife), then returns to his ashram. Only after those three have grown old and died does he speak the Bharata among humans, telling his pupil Vaishampayana to recite it at Janamejaya's sacrifice between ritual tasks. Sizes given: 100,000 verses with sub-stories, 24,000 without (called Bharata), a 150-verse index, and a separate 6,000,000-verse version: 3,000,000 for the gods, 1,500,000 for the ancestors, 1,400,000 for the gandharvas (celestial musicians) and 100,000 for humans. Narada told the gods, Asita Devala the ancestors, Shuka the gandharvas, yakshas (nature spirits) and rakshasas (demons), and Vaishampayana the humans.

**The two trees (vv. 110-111).** See the quoted verses under Manana.

**Summary of the story (vv. 112-140).** Pandu kills a sage in deer form during intercourse and is cursed. Kunti and Madri invoke Dharma, Vayu, Indra and the Ashvins. After Pandu's death, sages bring the boys to Dhritarashtra's court and vanish. People argue over whether they can be Pandu's sons, since he died long ago, until an unseen voice declares they are, with flowers and conch sounds. The people love Yudhishthira's purity, Bhima's steadiness, Arjuna's valour and the twins' humility. Arjuna wins Draupadi and carries the Rajasuya (royal consecration sacrifice), which needs the deaths of Jarasandha and Shishupala. Gifts pass through Duryodhana's hands, and his envy grows. In Maya's hall he stumbles, and **Bhima** mocks him in Krishna's presence. He wastes away. Dhritarashtra permits the dice game. Krishna hears and is angry, yet "approved of the disputes" and overlooked the gambling. The translator's bracket explains this as Krishna wanting the wicked kings destroyed.

**Dhritarashtra's lament (vv. 141-218).** After the war he tells Sanjaya he never wanted it and made no distinction between his sons and Pandu's. He is blind, weak and attached, and grieved along with Duryodhana. He will prove himself "prajna-chakshu" (one whose eye is wisdom). Then come some sixty verses ending "then I had no hope of victory, Sanjaya". Each names one moment: Draupadi's svayamvara, Subhadra's abduction, Khandava, the lac house, Jarasandha, the Rajasuya, Draupadi dragged in one cloth while menstruating, Duhshasana failing to reach the end of the clothes, the exile, Pashupata from Shiva, Indra's heaven, Virata, seven akshauhinis (army divisions), Krishna's whole-hearted commitment, Narada saying Krishna and Arjuna are Nara-Narayana, the failed peace mission, the plan to seize Krishna and his many forms, Krishna showing the worlds in his body to Arjuna, then every death in the war up to the sleeping massacre and Ashvatthama's weapon on Uttara's womb. Ten survive: three on his side (Kripa, Ashvatthama, Kritavarma) and seven on the other (Krishna, Satyaki, the five Pandavas). Eighteen akshauhinis are gone. He faints and wants to die.

**Sanjaya's reply (vv. 222-251).** Sanjaya lists 24 kings Narada once named to console a king grieving his son, then a much longer roll of earlier kings. All were greater, all died. Your sons were wicked, angry and greedy, so do not grieve them. You know your own partiality. No one outwits what must be. Time makes and unmakes everything.

**The text's claim for itself (vv. 252-275).** Sanjaya steadies the king, and on this Vyasa built this "Upanishad" (the Bharata). It sings Vasudeva, the eternal Brahman, source of all, seen in the heart by yogis as a reflection in a mirror. Reading this chapter daily removes sin; even one quarter-verse helps. It is the chapter that is the true body of the Bharata, best among histories as butter among curds. Purana and itihasa (history) must expand the Veda, and the Veda fears the man of little learning. The gods weighed it against the four Vedas and it was heavier, hence "Maha-bharata" (great weight). The chapter ends on purity of intent.

**Differs from the popular version:**
- The Maya's hall mockery here is by Bhima. The popular line where Draupadi laughs and says "son of a blind man" does not appear in this chapter.
- Krishna is said to have approved of the quarrels and ignored the dice. Popular telling has him simply absent and unaware.
- The disrobing verse names only Duhshasana and the endless pile of cloth. It does not mention Krishna or a prayer.
- Ganesha's condition and Vyasa's counter-condition are here, but the popular broken tusk used as a pen is not.
- Vyasa composes first and Brahma suggests the scribe, so the popular "Ganesha volunteers" is not what this says.
- Draupadi's birth from fire, Karna's parentage and Krishna on the battlefield teaching the Gita are absent. The Gita appears only as Arjuna despairing and being shown the worlds.

**Unclear, flagged:**
- v. 52 Sanskrit reads "manvadi" (beginning from Manu), but the Hindi says the text starts from "Narayanam namaskritya". I cannot tell from these pages what "Manu" refers to.
- v. 88: the Hindi calls Sabha and Aranya bird-hollows. The word in the verse (vitanka) I would want checked. I kept the Hindi.
- v. 89: "Arani Parva" is not a parva name in this volume's contents. Not explained on these pages.
- v. 103: the Hindi says 150 verses for the index, yet the chapter itself has 282. Not explained.
- v. 230: the grieving king's name reads "Shraitya" in OCR, possibly Shvaitya. I did not check the image.
- vv. 225-229: 24 kings only if Kakshivan and Aushija count as two, as the Hindi takes them.

### Manana

#### Episode: Vyasa, Brahma and Ganesha

- **L1 Theological:** Brahma and Ganesha both serve this book, yet Ugrashrava salutes Vishnu as Brahman before beginning. The Divine here is a source that lets itself be written. Even the all-knowing god has to pause, and that pause is what the verses are built to cause.
- **L2 Adhyatmika:** "Write nothing you have not understood" is a rule for me. Speed of intake is Ganesha's condition and comprehension is Vyasa's. My own reading lives inside that bargain: keep moving, but never pass what I did not grasp.
- **L3 Psychological:** Vyasa's first feeling after finishing is not triumph but worry about transmission. Ganesha's condition reads like a craftsman's terms. Vyasa's reply is a clever author's. Both protect their dignity without conflict.
- **L5 Literary:** Adbhuta rasa (wonder), lightened with play. Vyasa knots the verses "out of curiosity" (kutuhalat), the same word used for Ugrashrava's coming. A contest between author and scribe makes a vast text feel lively.
- **L6 Historical & textual:** This book prints the scene as main text. The 8,800 figure, the 24,000 and the 100,000 together also suggest a text aware of its own growth.

Verse (v. 81, p. 39): *aṣṭau ślokasahasrāṇi aṣṭau ślokaśatāni ca / ahaṃ vedmi śuko vetti saṃjayo vetti vā na vā* — "Eight thousand eight hundred verses: I know them, Shuka knows them, and Sanjaya may or may not."

#### Episode: The two trees

- **L1 Theological:** Krishna is placed at the root of the dharma tree, along with the Veda and the Brahmins. He is not a branch on the winning side but what the tree stands on. The note on p. 82 makes him the root of dharma's firmness.
- **L2 Adhyatmika:** The translator's note on p. 82 gives the inner reading: anger is the tree, ignorance its root, and one should cut the root early. Both trees grow in me. The useful question is which one I watered today.
- **L3 Psychological:** Calling the blind king the root is a hard diagnosis. Duryodhana is the visible fury, but it rests on an unwise parent who could not refuse him. Kin are not just villains and heroes; they are a system.
- **L4 Ethical:** The verse labels Dhritarashtra "amanishi" (unwise), not evil. It hints that lack of self-mastery in the person with authority can do more harm than open malice. The note on p. 82 says everything could have been avoided had he restrained his son.
- **L5 Literary:** Two perfectly matched verses, line for line. The symmetry tells me at the start that this is argument as much as story. Shanta (calm) framing over raudra (fury) content.
- **L6 Historical & textual:** The note on p. 82 is the translator's commentary, not Vyasa's text. Keep the two apart.

Verses (vv. 110-111, p. 43): *duryodhano manyumayo mahādrumaḥ / skandhaḥ karṇaḥ śakunis tasya śākhāḥ / duḥśāsanaḥ puṣpaphale samṛddhe / mūlaṃ rājā dhṛtarāṣṭro 'manīṣī* — "Duryodhana is a great tree made of anger; Karna the trunk, Shakuni its branches, Duhshasana its rich flower and fruit; the root is the unwise King Dhritarashtra."

*yudhiṣṭhiro dharmamayo mahādrumaḥ / skandho 'rjuno bhīmaseno 'sya śākhāḥ / mādrīsutau puṣpaphale samṛddhe / mūlaṃ kṛṣṇo brahma ca brāhmaṇāś ca* — "Yudhishthira is a great tree made of dharma; Arjuna the trunk, Bhimasena its branches, Madri's sons its rich flower and fruit; the root is Krishna, the Veda and the Brahmins."

#### Episode: Krishna and the dice (vv. 138-140)

- **L1 Theological:** This is the hardest point in the chapter. Krishna is angry and yet permits, which is a God who does not stop what he could. The translator's bracket supplies a purpose, destruction of adharmic kings, but the verse itself says only that he approved and overlooked.
- **L2 Adhyatmika:** Perhaps what looks like divine silence is not absence. I should not lean on that as comfort until I have read how the text itself develops it.
- **L4 Ethical:** Dharma-sukshma at its sharpest: can you be blameless if you see great wrong and hold back for a larger end? I cannot resolve this from one chapter and leave it open.
- **L5 Literary:** Told in two lines, with no drama. The flatness makes it more unsettling.
- **L6 Historical & textual:** The explanation in brackets is the translator's addition, and it shapes how a reader takes the verse. Worth comparing with the Sabha chapters when I get there.

#### Episode: Dhritarashtra's lament

- **L1 Theological:** As the lament proceeds, Krishna turns from a person into the reason: Nara-Narayana, the one whose single stride held the earth, the many-formed. A blind enemy gives one of the fullest theologies here, and it is fear that teaches him.
- **L2 Adhyatmika:** The refrain admits he knew each time and went on anyway. That is the pattern of my own avoidances: I see the sign, I note it, and I do not change. Knowing is not the same as turning.
- **L3 Psychological:** He opens with self-defence ("I never wanted this"), and the list itself refutes him. Sixty verses of "I knew" add up to a confession. He grieves, excuses and calls himself wise-eyed all in one breath.
- **L4 Ethical:** He claims no difference between his sons and Pandu's, while Sanjaya later names his cruelty and partiality to his face. Each warning was a chance to act, and a king had the power to. Hopelessness was not the same as helplessness.
- **L5 Literary:** Karuna rasa (grief) through refrain. The repeated line works like a drum, and the whole war is told through a blind listener receiving news. It also gives the plot away, so that the tension moves from what happens to how.
- **L6 Historical & textual:** Some refrain verses are printed in brackets as southern additions (for example Kichaka, Lomasha). The order isn't strictly chronological: the hope "ends" sixty times, which is rhetorical.

Refrain (vv. 150 onward, seen on p. 50): *tadā nāśaṃse vijayāya saṃjaya* — "then, Sanjaya, I had no hope of victory."

#### Episode: Sanjaya on time

- **L1 Theological:** Kala (time) is near-absolute here. The Hindi of v. 248 adds that the Paramatma (Supreme Self) calms time, but the Sanskrit only says time calms time. The chapter elsewhere calls Vasudeva the source of all, so the connection is implied, not stated.
- **L2 Adhyatmika:** "Time stays awake among the sleeping." Whatever I postpone, the clock isn't postponing. This need not be bleak; it is what makes today count.
- **L3 Psychological:** Sanjaya consoles by hard truth, not comfort: you were partial, your sons were wicked, kings greater than them died too. Scale is his medicine. Yet he also says it had to be, which lets the king partly off the hook.
- **L4 Ethical:** A real tension: Sanjaya names the king's fault (v. 245) and then says no one can avoid what must be (v. 246). Fate as consolation, not as excuse, seems to be the line, and it is thin.
- **L5 Literary:** Shanta rasa arrives after karuna. The long lists of dead kings are not padding; their sheer length produces the feeling they describe.
- **L6 Historical & textual:** The Hindi of v. 247 adds "the fruit of one's own karma", which is not in the Sanskrit words on the page; the verse names the ordainer's path and time. Translator's gloss, noted.

Verse (v. 248, p. 77): *kālaḥ sṛjati bhūtāni kālaḥ saṃharate prajāḥ / saṃharantaṃ prajāḥ kālaṃ kālaḥ śamayate punaḥ* — "Time creates beings, time destroys creatures; time in turn quiets the time that destroys."

#### Closing verse: purity of intent

- **L4 Ethical:** The chapter ends not on the war but on bhava (inner disposition). The same act is clean or corrupted by intent, and the note says this applies to reading the book too.
- **L6 Historical & textual:** Here the translation departs noticeably. *prasahya vittāharaṇam* in the Sanskrit plainly suggests seizing wealth by force, while the Hindi renders it as "wealth earned with hardship". Flagged, since the two readings say quite different things.

Verse (v. 275, p. 81): *tapo na kalko 'dhyayanaṃ na kalkaḥ / svābhāviko vedavidhir na kalkaḥ / prasahya vittāharaṇaṃ na kalkas / tāny eva bhāvopahatāni kalkaḥ* — "Austerity is no stain, study is no stain, the Vedic rule natural to one's station is no stain, taking wealth by force is no stain; these same things, struck by a corrupted disposition, are the stain."
